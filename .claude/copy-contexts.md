# Copy Contexts — Reference and Worked Examples

*Last reviewed: 02 June 2026*

Five primary contexts for MSS and client copy. Each has a different temperature — read the
relevant section before writing.

---

## 1. First Impression
*Website homepage, LinkedIn about section, referral context. Someone encountering the
studio or client for the first time.*

**Register:** Direct, clear, empathetic. States the offer without decorating it, but opens
with the reader's experience first.

**What it must do:**
- Open with the client's experience, not the studio's offer
- Establish who this is for immediately
- State what gets built or delivered
- Signal the quality of thinking without explaining it

**What to avoid:**
- Adjective-heavy openers ("passionate", "dedicated", "innovative")
- Process-led framing ("our approach involves...")
- Anything that could describe any studio
- Leading with capability before empathy

**Worked example (MSS):**
> Some things are hard to say not because they're unclear, but because no one has ever
> given them the right form. Main Stage Studio works with people who have a
> vision worth expressing. We listen until we understand it properly. Then we build
> everything around it, the brand, the language, the digital presence, so that when people
> encounter your work, they hear exactly what you meant.

*What it's doing: Opens with the client's experience, not the studio's offer. The empathy
comes first, the capability follows. No adjectives doing the work that substance should do.*

---

## 2. Discovery Framing
*Opening a client conversation, onboarding copy, engagement kickoff. Setting the tone
for how the work happens.*

**Register:** Warm but purposeful. A genuine opening, not a sales pitch.

**What it must do:**
- Position the process without explaining the process
- Turn the methodology into a question or invitation
- Make the client feel seen before anything is made
- Signal that the real story is worth finding, even if it isn't in the brief

**What to avoid:**
- Process diagrams dressed as copy ("Phase 1 involves...")
- Selling the methodology instead of opening the conversation
- Warmth without direction

**Worked example (MSS):**
> Before we talk about what anything looks like, I want to understand what you're actually
> trying to say. Most briefs are a starting point. The real story is usually underneath it,
> and it takes a proper conversation to find it. So let's start there. What made you build
> this?

*What it's doing: Positions the process without explaining the process. "The real story is
usually underneath it" signals empathy without sentimentality. The question at the end
opens a real conversation rather than a sales pitch.*

---

## 3. Case Study
*Portfolio entries, project write-ups. Demonstrating the work and the thinking behind it.*

**Register:** Narrative, grounded, specific. The client's world first, the studio's
contribution second.

**What it must do:**
- Lead with the client's situation, not the studio's process
- Make the problem feel real before the solution is introduced
- Make the client feel recognised, not just described — the reader should see themselves
  in the opening, not just the subject
- Let the work speak — resist over-explaining what was done

**Structure:**
1. Open with the client's world — who they are, what they were trying to do
2. Surface the real problem (which is rarely the brief)
3. Show the thinking that shaped the approach
4. Describe the outcome in terms the client would use, not studio language

**What to avoid:**
- "We were approached by..." (leads with the studio, not the client)
- Listing deliverables as if they're achievements ("We designed a logo, created a colour
  palette, and built a website")
- Vague outcomes ("the client was delighted with the results")

**Worked example (MSS):**
> Ayesha knew her audience. She'd been building relationships with them for years — in
> person, through referrals, through the quality of what she did. What she didn't have was
> a way to show people who hadn't met her yet. That's where we started.

*What it's doing: Leads with the client's world, not the studio's process. The problem
emerges from the person, not from a brief. "That's where we started" is the hinge — it
moves naturally into the work without a clunky transition. The reader recognises someone
they know in Ayesha before the studio has said a word about itself.*

## 4. Editorial Voice
*Studio point of view — site editorial section, LinkedIn articles, design thinking pieces.*

**Register:** Direct, opinionated, grounded in thinking. A position stated clearly.

**What it must do:**
- State a position — not "we think" or "in our view", just the position
- Develop the idea with enough substance to earn the opening claim
- Close with weight, not a summary
- Ground the position in lived experience of the tension between creative vision and
  execution, not just design theory

**What to avoid:**
- Hedged opinions ("it could be argued that...")
- Conclusions that restate the opening
- Performed intellectualism — if the idea doesn't hold up plainly stated, it doesn't hold up
- General design commentary that could come from anyone

**Worked example (MSS):**
> Most brand work starts with the wrong question. "What should this look like?" is asked
> before "What is this actually for?" The visual decisions are easy once the thinking is
> clear. The thinking is the hard part — and it's the part that usually gets skipped.

*What it's doing: States a position directly. No hedging, no "we think". The rhythm is
deliberate — short sentence lands the point, the next two develop it, the last one closes
with weight. The position comes from a specific place, not from design theory in the
abstract.*

---

## 5. Proposal / Direct Client Communication
*Proposals, scope confirmations, engagement framing. Business communication with a human on
the other end.*

**Register:** Confident, clear, no oversell. Trusts the reader.

**What it must do:**
- State what happens and in what order
- Explain the logic briefly — not exhaustively
- Sound like a person, not a document

**What to avoid:**
- Formal proposal language ("pursuant to our discussion...")
- Over-explaining every decision
- Hedging the scope ("approximately", "roughly", "we'll aim to")

**Worked example (MSS):**
> Here's how I see the engagement. We start with a discovery session — two hours, no
> agenda, just conversation. From there I'll put together a positioning document that we'll
> work through together before anything gets designed. Once that's locked, we move into
> brand expression. The sequence matters — each stage informs the next.

*What it's doing: Confident and clear without being transactional. "Here's how I see it"
rather than "our process involves." The last line explains the logic without
over-explaining — it trusts the reader to understand why sequence matters once it's named.*

---

## Notes on register

The five contexts above sit on a spectrum from warmest (discovery framing) to most direct
(proposal). The voice character stays constant across all of them. What shifts is:

- **Sentence length** — editorial and proposal copy runs shorter; case studies can breathe more
- **Personal pronouns** — "I" for direct client communication; "we" for studio voice; case
  studies can use either depending on the client relationship
- **Questions** — discovery framing uses them deliberately; other contexts rarely do
- **Empathy** — present in all contexts, but most explicit in first impression and discovery
  framing; in case studies it comes through specificity rather than statement

When in doubt about register, ask: what does the reader need to feel at the end of this?
Answered, that usually resolves the question.
