# Copy Contexts: Reference and Worked Examples

*v6, 10 Oct 2026. Companion to `mss-tone-of-voice.md`. v1 examples are archived in `_archive/brand-v1/`.*
*Same character in every context, different temperature. Every example below uses "I" and contains no em dashes.*

---

## 1. First impression
*Home page, LinkedIn intro, a referral email. Someone meeting MSS for the first time.*

> Most small businesses rent their own front door. The designer you have to email to change a word. Four logins to send one newsletter. The agency that launched it and kept the keys.
>
> I build the brand, the website, the content and the systems behind them, then hand you the controls. Prices are on the page.

**What it does:** names the problem in things the reader has lived, not in adjectives. Gets to what I build within two sentences. Ends on something checkable (prices on the page).

---

## 2. Product page
*One of the six products. The reader is deciding whether this is for them.*

> **Website.** A fast site you change yourself. Typo on a Sunday night? Fix it. No ticket, no invoice.
>
> Ten pages, copy written in your voice, live editing, search basics, launch and domain handled. The code lives in your own repository, so you can leave whenever you like and take it with you.
>
> 3 to 5 weeks · £540 founding · £1,800 target

**What it does:** nouns first, then the promise that makes it different (you own it), then the facts in mono. No "solutions", no "digital presence".

---

## 3. Case study
*Leads into a piece of work. The client's world first, then the work, then the receipt.*

> PureMed is one practitioner and a front desk. Before, changing a price on the website meant emailing someone and waiting.
>
> Now the owner edits her own pages, writes and approves her own emails, and keeps every client record in one place she controls. The site has been live on puremed.uk since 30 September 2026.

**What it does:** starts with the client's situation, not the studio's process. The proof is behaviour the reader can picture. Any figure added later passes `make figures` first. If the client has said something, quote her rather than describe her.

---

## 4. Editorial (journal)
*A studio point of view, published under the founder's name.*

> Agencies sell the launch. It's the easy part: a deadline, a reveal, a round of applause. The hard part is month three, when the person who built it has moved on and nobody can change a word without a ticket.
>
> I'd rather build things you can run without me. If you keep paying me, it should be because it's good, not because you're locked in.

**What it does:** states a position plainly, takes a side, and stays on the reader's side while doing it. Short sentence lands the point; the next ones earn it.

---

## 5. Proposal and direct client communication
*Scope, price, next steps. First person, warm, no padding.*

> Here's what I'd build. A Website first: ten pages, your words, live editing. Then, once it's running, a Campaign around your guide, because that's where your enquiries come from.
>
> Founding price for the Website is £540. I'll show you everything on one private link before it's real, and nothing goes live until you give the cue.

**What it does:** says what, why and how much in the order a client asks. Uses the cue line once, where it means something.

---

## Notes on register
- **Temperature, not character, changes.** The editorial piece can carry more swagger; the proposal carries more care. Both are plain.
- **Clips and demos** follow principle 7: narration only says what the screen shows. The clip recorder enforces "no em dashes"; the rest is on the writer.
- **Client copy uses the client's voice,** from their own tone-of-voice file, never this one.
