## 1. The bet

v4 bet the flagship story on the (anonymised) combat-sports content operation and aimed sales at clinics. On review, that has a translation gap: sports throughput and speed proves a different thing (can you produce fast, under pressure) than what a clinic buyer is actually nervous about (will this get me in trouble, do patients trust it). The founder would also be selling into a sector he has no personal track record in, learning the industry's operating rhythm at the same time as trying to sell into it.

v5 changes the axis the flagship proof runs on, from **operational throughput** to **regulatory credibility**, and picks a vertical where that credibility is real and already checkable (LinkedIn), not constructed for the pitch:

> **The founder has spent 20+ years in product delivery: a decade building and launching broadcast technology systems internationally (2003-2013), then roughly thirteen years across UK financial services, digital banking product delivery at a major retail bank, building a core banking platform used by banks to launch new digital propositions, consumer credit onboarding at scale (10M+ customers), and now onboarding for a regulated wealth platform. MSS is that same judgment, applied to a small regulated firm's digital presence and content, delivered through an AI-accelerated build pipeline.**

One sentence of positioning logic: *"I've spent thirteen years building and running onboarding for regulated financial products. Your firm's content and site are not the hard part of my week."*

**Career facts behind this (source: the founder's own CVs, not invented, employer names below stay internal-only and are never used in MSS marketing per the boundary above):**
- Broadcast technology, ~10 years (2003-2013): transmission/playout operations → broadcast systems implementation → managing EMEA channel launches (Benelux, Poland, Romania, Singapore, Sub-Saharan Africa) at a major broadcaster.
- Financial services, ~13 years (2013-present):
  - A major UK retail bank, Barclays (2013-2017): retail and Premier operational services, led the launch of an industry-first video banking proposition, then product-owned a digital savings product (the Lifetime ISA work referenced in conversation sits here).
  - A core-banking-platform vendor (2017-2024, ~6.3 years): product-managed onboarding, identity, and customer-interaction capabilities for a platform banks license to launch new digital retail banking propositions from scratch, "a bank in a box." Owned onboarding/identity for 3.5M active customers and ~50k new registrations/month; led the platform decision to replace ForgeRock with Amazon Cognito, cutting $1m/year in operating costs.
  - A consumer credit business (2024-2025, ~1.5 years): owned acquisition/onboarding journeys for 10M+ customers across partner and aggregator credit brands, supporting a sustained £150k/month revenue uplift.
  - A regulated wealth management platform (Sept 2025-present, ~11 months, current role): senior PM for platform onboarding across investor and adviser-firm journeys, reduced time-to-investment by 50%, digitised KYC/identity verification (SmartSearch, Experian), removing 5,000 hours/year of manual effort.

This is a materially different (and stronger) claim than "a decade in wealth onboarding": it is thirteen years of onboarding, identity, and regulated product delivery *across* financial services, with the wealth-specific slice being the most recent and current chapter, not the whole decade. The "See the machine" instinct from v4 has a real analogue here too, most of these achievements are quantified and checkable in outline on LinkedIn (time-to-investment, cost reductions, customer volumes), which is exactly the kind of concrete, numbers-led proof v4 could only gesture at anonymously.

This is a credibility swap, not just a market swap: the proof asset moves from an anonymised client operation to the founder's own personal, publicly-verifiable career. That closes the biggest weakness in v4 (an anonymised story persuades less than a named, checkable one) at the cost of the biggest strength in v4 (a live, dramatic, showable operation). See §8.

## 3. ICP v5

**Primary buyer:** small or boutique regulated/trust-sensitive professional services firms, sized closer to the founder's own scale than to enterprise. Financial advisers (IFAs), boutique wealth managers, insurance brokers/intermediaries, accountants, legal practices, and early-stage regulated or semi-regulated fintechs. The filter is **regulated or trust-sensitive**, not any single vertical inside that set, so the vertical bet's sales-cycle benefit (one voice, one compliance posture, repeat pattern) is kept without narrowing to a single industry the founder has to newly learn.

Why this wallet:
- These firms live under real compliance exposure (FCA rules, professional-body rules, GDPR, sector-specific disclosure requirements) and are typically too small to have in-house marketing/compliance-literate content capability, the same structural gap v4 identified in clinics, but here the founder can actually assess and speak to the specific risk rather than approximating it.
- The founder's day-job vocabulary (client categorisation, suitability, disclosure, KYC/AML, consent, data controllership) maps close to 1:1 onto what these buyers worry about in their own client-facing content and onboarding flows.
- Budgets clear a real retainer for the same reason clinics did in v4: recurring, compliance-sensitive content and presence needs, and genuine cost/risk of getting it wrong or going dark.

**Flagship proof (what earns the sale):** the founder's personal career, stated honestly and specifically per §1's career facts (20+ years product delivery; ~13 years across UK financial services spanning retail banking, a core-banking platform used to launch new digital banks, consumer credit onboarding at scale, and now a regulated wealth platform; a decade before that building and launching broadcast technology systems internationally), never naming any employer, never showing employer work product.

**Secondary proof (what shows the machine works):** actual delivered studio work, FSC (anonymised, per the existing rule) and PureMed, shown as evidence the AI-accelerated pipeline produces real, live, on-brand output, not as sector-matched case studies. These prove *execution*; the founder's bio proves *judgment*. The site needs both, doing different jobs.

**Values filter (survives from v1/v3):** intent over industry, unchanged.

## 4. Positioning

**Hero line (keep from v3/v4):**
> You don't need a bigger team. You need a better system.

Still holds, and sharpens with the Systems line added in §5.2: "a better system" stops being only a metaphor for MSS's own AI-accelerated content pipeline and becomes something literally buyable, the client's own operational system. That's a more literal, and more defensible, reading of the line than content alone ever gave it.

**The proof move (revised from v4):** v4's centre of gravity was a demonstration ("watch the machine run"). v5's centre of gravity is a credential backed by a demonstration: lead with *why the founder is safe to trust with something that matters*, then show the machine as evidence the trust is well placed. **The differentiator promise (unchanged from v4):** *the system does the labour, you keep the moment.* Still true, still the cleanest separation from "AI content" competitors.

**The AI story (unchanged from v3/v4):** method, not message. One honest paragraph.

## 5. Offer architecture — two service lines

Added 4 Aug 2026: MSS runs as **two service lines under one studio**, not one line with an extra tier. Both are credible from the same founder credential (§3), but they are sold differently and should not be blended into one package menu.

### 5.1 Presence — the existing trio, unchanged in shape

Fixed-scope, packaged, retainer-driven. v3's shape carries forward with names genericised off clinics.

- **Launch** — brand + Astro site + voice, fixed-scope build, architected to hand into a monthly. Unchanged in shape from v3/v4.
- **Presence Monthly** (renamed from "Clinic Monthly") — planned content calendar in voice, compliance-checked against the client's specific regulatory frame (not a single fixed pillar set, since "financial adviser" and "insurance broker" and "accountant" have different disclosure/promotion rules), reviewed and approved in Stage. Band locked in §9: £1,200–£1,800/mo founding-client rate, rising toward the standard £1,500–£3,000/mo Run band once proven.
- **Campaign** — time-boxed sprint, natural trial that converts to Monthly. Unchanged.

### 5.2 Systems — new, separate line

Config-driven, deterministic, potentially bespoke operational architecture for regulated firms: client/case journey design, policy-gate logic, onboarding or intake workflow tooling. The direct commercial expression of the §3 credential, not a demonstration of it, a buyer purchases the same judgment that shaped onboarding for millions of customers, applied to their own operation.

- **Not packaged.** "Potentially bespoke" is incompatible with a fixed-price menu. Sold through a discovery-then-proposal motion (scoping conversation, option paper, fixed-fee or day-rate quote per engagement), closer to how the founder's day job actually runs (business cases, option papers, scoped delivery) than to how Presence sells.
- **Deterministic, config-driven by construction, same discipline already proven in Studio** (client configs, not forked code, across FSC/PureMed/Vera), applied to operational logic instead of content pipelines: the output is a parameter-driven system (rules, gates, branching journeys), not a one-off script.
- **No public proof asset yet.** The closest real evidence, the founder's own wealth-onboarding journey architecture and use-case register, sits too close to the current employer's actual product to ever be shown publicly, same anonymisation boundary as everything else (§3, §8). Systems launches on credential alone until a first client engagement exists to reference.
- **Higher stakes than Presence.** A bug in a deterministic client-journey system for a regulated firm is a different order of consequence than a missed content post. Needs the same scope-of-responsibility discipline already flagged for compliance-checked content (v4 §8 risk 3), applied more strictly: a clear contractual line on what MSS is and isn't accountable for before this is ever sold as a compliance-safe system.

Layer 2 (self-serve vertical product tier) and Layer 3 (flagship-as-revenue) from v4 §5 stay deferred, unrelated to Systems, they were about productising Presence at scale, not about a second service line, and still depend on a live client operation to productise around.

## 8. Honest risks

1. **No delivered regulated-services client yet.** The founder's credential is real; a sector-matched *studio* case study is not. Same structural gap v4 had with clinics (flagged there as risk #2), not solved, moved. Mitigation: the credential section is framed as personal expertise, not studio track record, so it doesn't overclaim; the first regulated-services client becomes the priority Work asset, same sequencing lesson as v4.
2. **Credential-led sites can read as a personal consultancy, not a studio.** Leading with "who's behind it" risks collapsing MSS into "hire this one person" rather than "buy this system," undercutting the whole "system, not just me" positioning. Mitigation: keep the credential section tightly scoped to *why regulated work is safe here*, then hand off immediately to the machine/system sections; do not let the bio expand into a personal-brand page.
3. **Employer-boundary discipline under a stricter test than before.** FSC anonymisation was already proven; anonymising the founder's own day job while still making the credential feel concrete and specific is a harder needle to thread (vague enough to protect the employer, specific enough to be persuasive). Draft the actual copy and pressure-test it before shipping, this is a copy-craft risk, not a strategy risk, but a real one.
4. **Narrower sales motion than v4 for the same solo-operator ceiling problem.** "Regulated professional services" is broader than clinics but still not as broad as v3's horizontal SMB. Layer 2/3 from v4 are deferred (§5.2), so there's currently no named path off the founder-hours ceiling. Acceptable for now (v4 had the same open problem), but flag it, don't let it go unaddressed indefinitely.
5. **Systems raises the stakes of the anonymisation boundary.** The founder's best possible proof asset for Systems, the actual wealth-onboarding journey architecture, can never be shown, it sits inside the current employer's product domain. Systems has to sell on credential alone for longer than Presence did, and a bad first engagement would damage the exact credibility the whole v5 bet rests on. Mitigation: do not actively sell Systems until a scope-of-responsibility contract clause exists (§5.2), and treat the first engagement as deliberately small.
6. **Two service lines is a heavier sell for a solo operator than one.** Presence and Systems have different sales motions (packaged vs scoped), different delivery risk profiles, and different proof requirements. Keeping them visually and narratively distinct on the site (§7) is necessary but not sufficient, watch for this becoming two half-attended businesses instead of one coherent one.

## 10. "Who's behind it" — drafted copy

Studio voice throughout, no "I". The bio sentence uses "someone who..." to match the device already locked in `mss-tone-of-voice.md`'s origin story ("The studio was built by someone who spent years being effective at things that weren't quite the right fit..."), extending established voice rather than inventing a new one. The track record is the message; no stats, no employer-by-employer list, no pointer to the current role specifically. (An earlier first-person, stat-led draft was rejected 4 Aug for reading as a CV/achievement list in the wrong register; not kept here.)

> **Who's behind it**
>
> Main Stage Studio runs on a proven track record, not a portfolio built to look the part. It was built by someone who spent years inside the parts of business that aren't allowed to get it wrong, onboarding systems used by millions of customers, industries where a mistake isn't a bad review, it's a breach.
>
> That discipline doesn't disappear because the work is now a website, a content calendar, or the system your business actually runs on. It's the same instinct, wherever it's aimed: understand exactly where the real risk sits before anything gets designed.
>
> None of it turns your presence into a compliance document, or your operations into red tape. It means the difference between what your regulator actually requires and what's just how things have always been done is already understood here, not something you'll need to explain twice.

**Open before this locks:**
- No specific employer, role, or figure is named or implied by era, deliberate, given v1's feedback. Confirm this is the right altitude, general enough to protect the boundary and avoid the CV feel, specific enough to still be believable.
- "Onboarding systems used by millions of customers" is the only quantifier left in the copy, kept because it's load-bearing for credibility. Flag if even this should go.
