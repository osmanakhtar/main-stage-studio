For most of my career, testing an idea meant writing about it first.

Thirteen years building onboarding for regulated financial products teaches you to respect process. It should. It also shows you how much time goes into describing a thing before anyone can see it. A requirements document. A walkthrough deck. A meeting to agree what the deck meant.

What's changed isn't the thinking. It's the distance between the thought and something you can click.

I now build small tools I fully expect to delete. A prototype to settle one decision. A script that checks one thing across every page. A mock journey to see where someone gives up. Each takes an afternoon, and each gets thrown away once it has answered its question.

It feels wasteful at first. It isn't. Building was never the expensive part. Being wrong for months, because nobody could see the problem until it shipped, was.

That's the thinking behind how we work at Main Stage Studio. The studio page has the longer version this week.

People hear "disposable tools" and assume I'm relaxed about risk. It's the opposite.

I've spent thirteen years in UK financial services, most of it on onboarding and identity. That's the part of a product where a wrong rule isn't a bug report. It's a customer who shouldn't have been let in, or one who should have been and wasn't.

That work gave me a line I still draw on everything.

Anything that answers a question can be quick and temporary. Prototypes, test harnesses, a mock journey in a browser. Build it, learn from it, bin it.

Anything a customer actually passes through can't. The live journey. The rule that decides which disclosure someone sees. The form that feeds an identity check. That gets built deliberately, from configuration, and it changes through review.

The failure I saw most wasn't moving too fast. It was moving at one speed. Either everything went through the heavy process, so nothing got tested early, or everything got the quick treatment, including the parts that couldn't afford it.

Two speeds. Know which one you're in.

If you run a regulated firm: where does that line sit for you?
